# Front Porch Tune-up

**The rule:** a local humanist group's website does its job when a stranger on
a phone can tell, at a glance, that the group is real and current, when and
where it meets next, and what to do next. Within 30 seconds they should also
know what walking in will be like.

The Front Porch Tune-up is a checklist for checking that, and for improving
it. It's not a test. It shows points, never "fail," and it's tailored to
each community by the people who know it.

Status: design draft, future item. Source: *Humanist Organizer Playbook*, AHA
Organizing Team, 2026 (Jake Via). Page numbers are the playbook's.

---

## No checklist fits every community

The best judge of a group's website is the local members who build it. This
tune-up is a frame for their knowledge, not a replacement for it.

Jake says it himself: "Every community can – and probably should – look a bit
different, even in the same city" (Introduction). Some examples:

- A group in a community where most people come from one religious
  tradition, whether Christian, Muslim or Jewish, whose site speaks to people
  leaving that tradition.
- A group whose members largely share an ethnicity or a language, whose site
  speaks to that community first, in its own language where that fits.
- A group that leads with the name of its region rather than a town, because
  everyone close enough to attend knows what it means. In California,
  "Tri-Valley" means San Ramon, Pleasanton and Livermore to anyone who lives
  there.

A generic checklist would mark all of these down. This one asks first.

## Before starting: three questions for the organizers

1. **What do your neighbors call where they live?** A town, a county, a region
   ("Tri-Valley"), or several. These are the place names the tune-up looks
   for.
2. **Who are you trying to reach, in your own words?** "Humanists, atheists,
   agnostics, and the friendly-curious" is the playbook's default (p.24). Use
   yours.
3. **Does any item not fit your community, and why?** See "Setting items
   aside."

Answering these is already half the value: it makes the organizers say out loud
who the site is for.

## How points work

- **Sections.** Two for now: **First screen** (60 points) and **First 30
  seconds** (40 points). More will come later (see "Later sections").
  - *Why:* visitors move in steps (first glance, then a scroll or a tap) and
    leave at the first step that doesn't work: "If any link in that chain is
    broken, Tuesday Night Person falls through it, and they usually don't try
    twice" (p.23). Counting each step separately shows *where* a site loses
    people. A single total hides it.
  - *Why the first screen carries more points:* it's the only part every
    visitor sees. The playbook's own fix starts there: "Fix the top of one
    page" (p.26).
- **Must-haves.** Each section lists a few items the playbook treats as
  essential. The tune-up shows them first, as a count: "Must-haves: 2 of 3."
  - *Why:* with points alone, strengths can make up for a missing essential,
    for example great photos and no next event. A real visitor never makes
    that trade. Listing must-haves first, as a count, keeps the essentials
    visible without calling anything a failure.
  - *Why so few:* each section has at most three, all things the playbook
    calls non-negotiable or the Stranger Test requires. More would make nearly
    every site look incomplete and stop pointing at what matters most.
- **Points.** Every item, must-have or not, carries points. Each section shows
  points earned out of points possible, for example "First screen: 41 of 60."
  The overall total is the sum across sections.
  - *Why points at all:* points show progress. A group that fixes four things
    should see the total move, even if one must-have is still missing.
- **Ratings.** Each section and the total get a rating, by share of possible
  points:

  | Share of possible points | Rating |
  |---|---|
  | under 50% | **Getting started** |
  | 50–69% | **Good** |
  | 70–84% | **Better** |
  | 85% and up | **Best** |

  **A section can't rate above Good until its must-haves are all there, and
  neither can the overall total.** That keeps a strong photo and button from
  hiding a missing next event, the one thing the playbook says matters most.
  - *Why a share rather than raw points:* set-aside items change a section's
    possible total, so groups are rated on what applies to them.
  - *Why "Getting started" rather than a failing grade:* without a rung below
    Good, a site with no events and no place name would rate Good, which is
    untrue. "Getting started" is honest and still encouraging.
  - *Why these bands:* 50 / 70 / 85 are a first guess. They'll be adjusted
    after trying it on real sites (see "Before trusting the numbers").
- **No negative language.** Nothing "fails." The tune-up lists what's
  there, what's next, and how many points each next step adds.
  - *Why:* the people reading the tune-up are volunteers. Pass/fail tells
    a group that fixed four of five things the same thing as a blank page,
    and that discourages exactly the people doing the work. The playbook's
    tone is the same: "No shame. Nearly every volunteer-run group on Earth
    has this exact website" (p.20). Next steps with points attached say what
    to do and what it's worth.

### Setting items aside

- **Must-haves can't be set aside.**
  - *Why:* they're what every newcomer needs, wherever they live. If they
    could be skipped, the total would stop meaning anything.
- **Any other item can**, with a one-line reason the tune-up displays, for
  example: "Photos: we don't show members' faces; several aren't out to family
  or employers."
  - *Why:* local members know things a checklist can't, such as a hostile
    town, a group too small for turnout numbers to help, or a group that stays
    off social media on purpose. The playbook itself warns that "some of your
    members cannot afford to be found" (p.41).
- A set-aside item's points come off the possible total, so it neither helps
  nor hurts.
  - *Why:* counting a set-aside item as zero would punish a deliberate,
    reasonable choice. Counting it as earned would reward skipping work.
- The tune-up shows how many items were set aside, and the reasons. That's
  transparency, not a judgment.
  - *Why:* showing the reason keeps setting items aside honest without anyone
    having to police it. A reason that doesn't hold up is visible to anyone
    who reads the tune-up.

### What "first screen" and "30 seconds" mean

- **First screen:** what a phone shows before any scrolling, measured at
  **360 × 640** CSS pixels. That's a Galaxy S25 (360 × 780) minus the browser's
  address and tool bars. Cookie banners and "get our app" pop-ups count; a real
  visitor sees them too.
- **First 30 seconds:** the playbook's "30 seconds" (p.20) can't be timed from
  a URL, so the tune-up uses a stand-in: **anything within the first three
  phone screens, or one tap from a link on the first screen.**

## Section 1: First screen (60 points)

Answers "Are you real and current? When and where? What do I do?" without a
single scroll.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Next event:** a future date and a place | ✓ | 15 | No | Automated: render at 360×640, look for a future date and a venue | p.20 |
| **Calendar is accurate:** no past event shown as upcoming | ✓ | 10 | No | Automated: compare dates on the page to today | p.19, p.21 |
| **Works on a phone:** no sideways scrolling, readable text, tappable buttons | ✓ | 10 | No | Automated: Lighthouse mobile checks | p.24 |
| **Place name your neighbors use** (from question 1) | | 6 | Yes | Automated: search for the organizers' place names | p.24 |

| **One clear next step:** one main button, no more than three competing | | 6 | Yes | Heuristic: count prominent buttons and links | p.20 |
| **A real photo of people** | | 4 | Yes | Human judgment; stock photos don't count | p.21 |
| **A sign of life:** last get-together, turnout, or something dated this month | | 2 | Yes | Automated: a date within the past 31 days | p.21 |

Answers the rest of the Stranger Test (p.26): "What would you wear? Would you go

## Heads-ups (no points)

Things worth knowing, shown neutrally and not counted in points.

*Why no points:* they're worth fixing but aren't about what a newcomer needs
to find. Some, like a visible home address, are about safety, and turning
safety into points invites trading it against other items. Listing them
separately keeps them in view without any of that.

- A private home address is visible publicly. The playbook says to share it
  only after an RSVP (p.43).
- Newest dated post is over a year old (the "Welcome to our new website!"
  pattern, p.19).
- More than three buttons compete on the first screen ("One button. Not nine,"
  p.20).
- A pop-up or banner covers the first screen.
- A linked social account hasn't posted in six months (p.27).

## Later sections

- **Accessibility.** Contrast, image descriptions, captions, keyboard use,
  screen-reader structure. "Accessibility is radical empathy applied to web
  design" (p.24). Parked, not dropped.
- **Porch upkeep.** Things a URL can't show: two admins on every account, a
  shared password manager, a Google Business Profile, someone with 15 minutes a
  month to keep the site current (p.21, p.24, p.42). Likely a checklist the
  organizers fill in rather than earning points.

The playbook gives no numeric weights, but it ranks things clearly. The
must-haves and point values follow its words; the exact numbers are ours, for
Jake and others to adjust.

## Before trusting the numbers

1. Run the tune-up on 8–10 real humanist group sites by hand.
2. Run a real Stranger Test (p.26) with a person on three of them.
3. Where the tune-up and the person disagree, change the tune-up.

## How to build it (cheapest first)

1. **A printable checklist.** These tables as a fill-in form. No code. Could
   ship with the playbook.
2. **A spreadsheet**, with the points, set-asides and ratings calculated.
3. **A web tool.** Asks the three questions, renders the page at 360×640, runs
   the automated checks, and asks the organizers about the judgment items.
4. **An AHA view across chapters**, showing each group only its own tune-up
   and trend.
